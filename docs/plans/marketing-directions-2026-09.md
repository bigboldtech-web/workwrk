# WorkwrK marketing site: three directions

2026-09. Research only. Nothing here is built. Pick one, then we build it.

**How to read the references.** Every screen is a link to Mobbin. Screens marked with a dagger (†) I opened and looked at in this pass. The rest come from the four competitor surveys, where the researcher states they opened each one. Nothing in this document is described from metadata or from memory. Where I believe something but have no screen for it, I say so.

**The fixed constraint, restated.** The product's own visual language is settled and shipped: navy rail and top bar, white canvas, light-grey sidebar, Inter, one blue primary per page. Wherever any of these three directions shows the product, it shows exactly that, unretouched, untilted, unrecoloured. This is not a compromise, it is what the whole serious end of the market already does. Across monday, ClickUp, Jira, Notion, Affinity and Harvey, every screenshot is the shipped UI with its own chrome and its own colours. Nobody redraws the product to match the marketing palette. Affinity is the cleanest proof: an expressive serif headline, a neutral pill tab group, and then a completely unmodified dark application screenshot that owes nothing to the page around it ([Affinity †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/2413e6dd-120f-418c-8355-710867916366)). And a dark page can hold a light product: incident.io places a light-chrome product frame on a near-black page without restyling it ([incident.io](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/1f8c9d4b-9f7f-4bfa-aed3-9a849b84831e)).

## Why the current site fails, in three findings

Worth stating plainly, because all three directions are shaped by it.

**1. The concept is not original, which is why it feels cheap.** The rejected hero is a row of module cards, each with an icon, a name and a line saying what it replaces, sitting above a product frame. That is Notion's homepage, card for card ([Notion](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/f7ed85df-523e-4081-9262-b89c14793bde)). The exploded-modules variant with the "all in one tool" claim is Linear's ([Linear](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/f8b93019-f62b-4e58-8f0d-e577baa204f3)). Restyling it does not fix it. The device has to change.

**2. The replacement argument is in the wrong place.** No rival makes it in the hero. ClickUp, the most aggressive consolidator alive, puts "REPLACES: Asana | Monday | Jira" in small caps far below the fold under a product section ([ClickUp](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/2e29fbe7-7bdd-4574-8b48-138f7770994f)). Notion and Ramp move it to fully designed comparison pages ([Notion vs Jira](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/e04ddef6-0dcc-47ef-b2aa-c5dcb98effaf), [Ramp vs Mercury](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/d5541dd9-5262-4788-aeda-0851057f2a4b)). The hero states an identity. The comparison page names the enemy.

**3. There is no proof ladder at all.** Every serious site climbs the same rungs in the same order: celled customer logos, a scale number, outcome numbers attributed to named companies, an analyst or peer position, certifications, an admin capability table, then downloadable evaluation assets. Mural runs the whole ladder ([celled logo grid †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/dfb72202-ae7b-4b55-8879-f68191fa6671), [Fortune 100 plus the enterprise feature table †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/694c88d0-ddce-4db0-942f-c7dbfd6c1a0b), [security on black †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/94a47d48-2f5d-41c8-99ba-19daba497e6e)). The current site runs none of them, and substitutes one anonymous anecdote in the eyebrow. Anonymous anecdotes appear above the fold on no rival site anywhere in this research.

Also worth recording: the "Tuesday" narrative has zero precedent. Not one rival hero tells a story, walks through a day, or names a fictional company. None of the three directions below uses one.

# Direction 1: THE SYSTEM OF RECORD

**One line.** Sell it the way Workday is sold: not a better tool for a team, but the governed system the whole company runs on, proved with the boring evidence a procurement committee actually reads.

## The argument

Your company already has fourteen places where work happens and no single place where it is on the record. WorkwrK is one system with one permission model, one audit trail and one administrator, covering the work, the documents, the processes and the people accountable for them. You buy it once for the whole organisation, not per team, and the security review, the access model and the rollout are our job, not yours.

## Real copy

**Hero headline:** The system of record for how your company works.

**Hero subhead:** One permission model, one audit trail, one administrator, covering projects, documents, processes, goals and the people accountable for them. Deployed org-wide, not team by team.

**Section headlines:**

- "Run it for one department. Govern it for all of them."
- "Everything your security review will ask, answered here."
- "Rollout is our job. Here is who does it and how long it takes."

Supporting lines, written, not described:

- Above the logo register: "IN PRODUCTION AT" in small letterspaced caps. Nothing else.
- Above the admin table: "Every control an administrator needs, listed. No adjectives."
- Enterprise plan column, one line: "For organisations that need SSO, provisioning, data residency and a contract."
- Closing band, two doors: "Talk to us about your organisation" and "Start with one team".

## What the hero shows

No product screenshot. The hero is a calm three-band composition on white: a small letterspaced caps eyebrow, the two-line headline at roughly two and a half times body size, one sentence of subhead, then a single dark "Talk to sales" primary with a plain "See the product" text link beside it. The image to the right is not a screenshot, it is a bordered register: a grid of hairline cells holding customer marks and, interleaved, outcome tiles carrying one number each. This is Retool's construction, where the logo wall, the outcome numbers and the compliance marks are one object instead of three bands ([Retool](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/5f2851eb-7ce8-449e-bbc1-440c7925ce1f)), and Vercel's, where the logo grid with three tiles lit as raised cards is itself the hero image ([Vercel](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/f875072a-1073-4dd1-b798-dbef0af45339)).

The product appears first at the fourth section, full width, at real density, under a tab strip labelled by objection rather than by feature: Security, Scale, Administration, Compliance. That is exactly how Jira Enterprise does it ([Jira Enterprise](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/464612ab-e548-4707-92d4-3c9445778c89)).

The top bar carries a published phone number and a "Chat with sales" link above the main nav, plus "Enterprise" as its own top-level nav item. Okta's utility strip is the reference ([Okta](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/06615e5e-64d6-47e4-834b-3de334337073)), and "Enterprise" as a top-level item is the single most consistent enterprise marker across the whole survey set.

## The visual language

White and light-grey alternating bands, one deep navy band used twice: once for the impact numbers and once for the closing CTA. Type is Inter throughout, one family, sentence case, headline at roughly two and a half times body, never a billboard. One accent, the product's own blue, spent only on the primary button and on hairline rules. Customer logos normalised to near-black at matched optical weight and set in bordered cells, never floating loose: Cursor's tiled field is the reference for how a wall of marks reads as a register rather than a sponsor page ([Cursor †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/1ffcbd94-da3c-4a55-a7ab-4f9311742312)). Compliance marks desaturated to the page, three to six maximum, never a badge row. Density is moderate to high: this direction is allowed to be dense, because density reads as substance in tables and registers. Whitespace is spent on the hero and the two navy bands, not on the middle of the page. Motion: almost none. A tab swap and a seat-count recalculation on the pricing table, nothing else.

Product chrome unchanged everywhere it appears. It sits flat in a plain white card with a small caption underneath naming the view, which is Jira's treatment ([Jira](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/777f617f-263e-4892-b045-9e95c67f2ac2)).

## Section order

1. Utility bar (phone, chat with sales, status) and nav with Enterprise as a top-level item
2. Hero: headline, subhead, two actions, the celled logo and outcome register
3. Scale line stated as a sentence, then the full logo wall
4. Outcome numbers, each one attributed to a named customer with role and company
5. Product under objection-named tabs: Security, Scale, Administration, Compliance
6. The suite as a plain bordered grid of named modules, one sentence each, no "replaces" lines
7. Administrator capability table: SSO and SAML, SCIM provisioning, roles and permission floors, audit logs, data residency, retention, export, IP allowlist
8. Certifications, small, desaturated, with one plain-language paragraph on data handling
9. Services and rollout: who implements, migration, training, named customer success, response times
10. Pricing: four columns with the recommended column tinted full height, rows grouped under band headers, Enterprise column ends in "Contact sales" not "Sign up"
11. Resources for the evaluation committee: platform overview, buyer's guide, ROI model, security whitepaper, API docs
12. Closing band on navy, two doors, each with its own sentence
13. Deep footer with a Legal and Trust column and a live status indicator

## Best for

The person who signs, not the person who clicks. CIO, COO, head of IT, procurement and security at a 1,000 to 10,000 seat organisation replacing an incumbent. It is also the only one of the three that survives a formal RFP and a vendor security questionnaire without improvisation.

## Risk

This is the honest one: **the ladder is currently a set of empty slots.** It needs named customer logos with permission to use them, at least one outcome number a real customer will stand behind, a completed SOC 2 or ISO audit, a documented data-residency story and a real services team with response-time commitments. We have none of those on the page today. Build this direction and the site immediately writes cheques the company has to cash, and a half-filled proof ladder reads worse than no ladder at all, because the gaps are conspicuous by convention.

Second risk: it is the most generic of the three. Run properly it is indistinguishable in register from Jira Enterprise, Airtable Enterprise and Mural, which is a compliment and a problem. It wins evaluations it is invited into. It does not create demand, and it gives a founder-led sales motion nothing memorable to lead with.

Third: it under-sells the one thing WorkwrK has that ClickUp and Monday do not, which is the people and alignment layer. In this direction that becomes row eleven of a capability table.

# Direction 2: OPEN THE PRODUCT

**One line.** Stop describing the product and show it, at full density, module by module, with a live workspace anyone can open without a form.

## The argument

Every tool in this category promises breadth and shows you a diagram. WorkwrK shows you the actual screens: the Space tree, the task with its dependencies, the SOP, the table, the goal, the org. Pick a module and look at it, then open a real workspace with real data in it and click around for as long as you like before you speak to anyone.

## Real copy

**Hero headline:** Every module. The actual screen. No mock-ups.

**Hero subhead:** Work, Planner, Docs, Tables, Talk, Teams and Goals are one product with one permission model and one data model. Pick one below, or open a live workspace and drive it yourself.

**Section headlines:**

- "Pick a module. This is the real screen, at real density."
- "Follow one task from the day it is assigned to the goal it moves."
- "Open a live workspace. No form, no call, nothing to install."

Supporting lines:

- Tab strip label, small caps: "SEVEN MODULES, ONE PRODUCT."
- Under the live workspace button: "Pre-loaded with a fictional 300-person firm. Nothing to set up, nothing saved."
- Above the admin section: "The same screens your administrator sees."

## What the hero shows

A horizontal pill tab strip sitting directly under a short left-ranged headline, and beneath it one full-width WorkwrK screen, flush to the page edges, untilted, no browser chrome, no drop shadow, no glow. Clicking a tab swaps the screen and nothing else on the page moves. The screen is shown large enough that the sidebar labels and the status pills are legible, because legibility is the argument.

Three verified constructions for exactly this. Affinity: expressive headline, neutral pill group, unmodified app screenshot ([Affinity †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/2413e6dd-120f-418c-8355-710867916366)). Superhuman: a four-tab strip where each tab is a named module carrying its own mark, opening onto a split panel of claim, checklist and product ([Superhuman †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/a35451f7-dc25-4ac8-a1f8-9867c48b4573)). monday's own HR page: a vertical tab list down the left with six named workflows beside one product shot, which suits a product with named modules better than a horizontal row ([monday.com †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/cf395119-4f40-496a-a656-b8c092a49d4d)).

The second door in the hero is the differentiator, and it is a product commitment, not a copy decision: a live, pre-seeded, read-write demo workspace behind one button. ClickUp gestures at this with "Explore the complete solution for everything work, no signup or credit card required" ([ClickUp](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/04fbd9b6-eea2-402b-b453-b4b50bd84fa8)). Nobody in the survey set actually delivers it from the hero. If we do, that is the whole advantage of this direction.

## The visual language

White page, near-monochrome chrome, all the colour inside the product frame where it already lives. The product sits on a light-grey plate (the same grey as the product's own sidebar) so its white canvas separates cleanly from the page. Type: Inter for everything, two sizes in the hero and no third tier. Tabs are neutral pills with the active one filled dark, which is the one piece of chrome allowed personality. One accent, the product blue, used on the single primary button only; the tabs are grey and black.

Density is high by design, because this direction's claim is depth. Whitespace is spent between sections, not inside them. The one place the page changes ground is a single navy band for the security and administration section, which lets a light product frame sit on dark without being restyled.

Captions matter here: every screenshot gets a small grey line naming the view, the way Jira captions "List view" and "Calendar" ([Jira](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/777f617f-263e-4892-b045-9e95c67f2ac2)). Where a screen is too dense to read whole, crop it and annotate with hairline callouts pointing at regions, which is Stripe's device ([Stripe](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/1306f939-95b2-4609-b941-59924fa26b28)) and Zipline's ([Zipline †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/ce7e2b2b-54f9-4a13-952e-4a895a0f7c30)).

Motion: tab swaps, a scroll-linked sequence for the single-task trace, and nothing else. No parallax, no floating cards.

## Section order

1. Nav with Product, Solutions, Pricing, Enterprise, Contact sales
2. Hero: headline, subhead, module tab strip, full-width real screen, live workspace button
3. Greyscale customer logos in cells, one row, immediately under the fold
4. "Follow one task": a scroll sequence tracing a single object across assignment, dependency, document, time, goal and review, using real screens at each step
5. The module grid, seven named modules, each a category word, a three-word claim and a real screen. No "replaces" lines. Notion's mosaic is the model for the structure, not the drawings ([Notion](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/8d3bf907-8d57-4fea-8da4-4890c90fd1ea))
6. Views strip: Table, Board, Calendar, Gantt, List, Gallery as chips that swap one screenshot
7. Role tiles: Ops, PMO, HR, Finance, Delivery, Leadership, each opening on the screens that role lives in, built like Amplemarket's audience tiles ([Amplemarket †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/85de217d-baba-4edb-8805-3d2a76f2244b))
8. Navy band: administration and security, with the real admin console shown light on dark
9. Named customer quote with a metric pulled out at three times the quote size
10. Pricing, then the comparison page link
11. Closing band: "Open a live workspace" primary, "Talk to sales" secondary
12. Footer with a Compare column

## Best for

The hands-on evaluator who runs the bake-off. Head of operations, PMO lead, the ops manager who has already trialled ClickUp and Monday and is tired of being shown diagrams. It is also the best direction for a founder-led sales motion, because the site and the demo become the same artefact.

It is by far the cheapest of the three to produce: the assets are screenshots of a product that already exists, and no illustration, photography or custom render is required.

## Risk

**A product tour is not an argument.** This direction shows what WorkwrK is and never says why it should win. If our screens look broadly like ClickUp's screens, the page proves parity, and parity always loses to the incumbent the buyer already has. It hands the visitor a feature comparison and trusts them to do the reasoning, which enterprise buyers largely will not.

Second risk, and it is operational: every screen is now a public commitment. A thin module, an empty state, a half-built Goals page or a Talk channel with three messages in it goes on the homepage. It also creates permanent maintenance debt, because the marketing site now has to be reshot every time the product changes, and the product is changing weekly across nine build phases.

Third: the live workspace is the whole advantage, and it is not a marketing task. It needs a seeded tenant, reset-on-schedule, rate limiting, abuse handling and a story for what happens when someone breaks it. If we ship this direction without the live workspace it collapses into a nicer-looking version of what ClickUp already does.

# Direction 3: THE CHAIN OF OWNERSHIP

**One line.** One sharp idea, argued with editorial restraint: WorkwrK is the only work system where every task is attached to the person accountable, the process it must follow and the goal it moves, so the performance record writes itself.

## The argument

In every project tool on the market a task is an orphan: it lives in a board, not in anybody's job. WorkwrK attaches each piece of work to an owner, to the standard it must meet, and to the goal it moves, so a manager can see what a person is accountable for and whether it happened. That is why at the end of a quarter the review is already written, and it is a thing ClickUp, Monday and Asana structurally cannot do.

## Real copy

**Hero headline:** Every piece of work knows who it belongs to.

**Hero subhead:** WorkwrK connects a task to the person accountable for it, the process it has to follow and the goal it moves. By the end of the quarter, the record of who delivered what already exists.

**Section headlines:**

- "Your project tool does not know what anybody's job is."
- "Write the process once. Every task that follows it is measured against the same standard."
- "The quarterly review, assembled from work that actually happened."

Supporting lines:

- Figure caption, mono: "FIG 1. Task, owner, standard, goal."
- Above the product frame: "This is the real screen. Nothing has been simplified for the picture."
- Closing band: "See it against your own org chart" and "Start with one department".

## What the hero shows

Warm off-white page. The headline is set very large and tight in a display face, ranged left across two lines, taking roughly a third of the viewport with nothing else in it. The subhead does not sit beneath the headline, it sits in a narrow column to its right at body size, which is what makes a page read composed rather than shouted. That asymmetry is Harvey's and Frontify's device ([Harvey](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/2e23b7a5-3438-48b3-ae2c-0b8e468e15a5), [Frontify](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/4304a783-e6de-4867-b2ff-51662137b505)). One dark "Book a walkthrough" button and one plain text link.

No product in the hero. Beneath the fold sits one drawn object: a hairline technical figure, in the page's own line weight, showing a single task rising through owner, standard and goal, with monospace leader labels and a mono figure caption. This is the one place the site draws anything, and it draws a model, not a feature list. Verified precedents for drawing a model at enterprise register: Mercury's labelled isometric layers ([Mercury †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/3a483a4e-ab5c-494e-9db9-c6d41660be22)), Cloudflare's hairline architecture canvas with named groups and corner registration marks ([Cloudflare Workers †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/3f43d4a8-c70c-4f82-a216-52bd0a30a932)), Rox's exploded dark layers with a left-hand layer index ([Rox †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/f81fe9bd-84ae-40e5-8ddd-6a406a3442fc)), and Linear's wireframe stack with mono leader labels ([Linear](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/f8b93019-f62b-4e58-8f0d-e577baa204f3)).

Important distinction from the rejected hero: this is not exploded module cards. Nothing in it is a product name, nothing says what it replaces, and it explains a relationship rather than listing an inventory.

The product appears once, later, and when it does it is enormous, honest and dense: a real WorkwrK screen at full density on a deliberate plate, with no crop and no apology. Harvey's Command Center frame is the exact treatment, and the page's restraint is what makes the density read as substance rather than clutter.

## The visual language

Two grounds only: a warm off-white and one deep navy plate used twice. A display face for headlines, high contrast and tight, paired with Inter for all body copy, plus a monospace face used only for figure captions, small-caps eyebrows and table labels. Three faces total, and the mono is what gives the page an engineering voice without a second palette.

One colour event per screen. The product's blue appears on the primary button and inside the product frame, nowhere else. No gradients. No illustration of people, ever, in this direction: the warmth comes from photography of real people in real rooms on the customer pages, which is how Stripe and Ramp handle it. Whitespace is extreme by our standards: the hero gets a third of the viewport and the figure gets a full band on its own.

Texture does the work that decoration usually does, and there are exactly two: a faint hairline grid with corner registration marks behind the figure sections, and a single textured plate under the product frame. Both are verified devices ([Cloudflare's hairline canvas †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/68e60c5c-d710-4b37-9901-384e2d76262a), [Vercel's graph-paper and crosshairs](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/918111cc-0e15-4a62-942b-57715e278a2a)).

Motion: one scroll-linked build of the ownership figure, drawn line by line, and nothing else on the page moves.

Product chrome unchanged. The navy rail sits against a warm off-white page, and that contrast is the composition, not a problem to be solved.

## Section order

1. Nav, minimal: Product, Why WorkwrK, Customers, Pricing, Enterprise, Contact sales
2. Hero: asymmetric headline and subhead column, one dark button, one text link
3. The figure: task, owner, standard, goal, drawn as one hairline technical diagram with a mono caption
4. The problem, stated in prose over a navy plate: "Your project tool does not know what anybody's job is." A short manifesto, three paragraphs, set at reading size, in the manner of Equals ([Equals †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/71913350-1721-470b-8e68-99818ad75518))
5. One product frame, full width, at real density, on a plate, with a caption
6. Ownership in practice: three bands, each one sentence and one cropped real screen. Who owns this. What standard it follows. What goal it moves
7. The review section: what a manager sees at quarter end, shown as a real screen, not a claim
8. Proof, compressed: one celled logo register and three numbers attributed to named customers. No badges
9. Security and administration, quiet, near-monochrome, one paragraph and one short capability list

11. Closing band on navy: two doors, each explained in a sentence
12. Footer, deep, with Legal and Trust and Compare columns

## Risk

**It leads with the least finished part of the product.** KRA, KPI, goals and reviews exist but are thinner than Spaces, Lists and Tasks, and this direction puts them on the homepage while relegating the daily-driver breadth to a band halfway down. If the walkthrough does not deliver what the hero promises, the gap is the story.

Second: it narrows the funnel deliberately. A PMO shopping for a project tool, which is the majority of inbound traffic in this category, will not see the words they searched for and will bounce. Direction 3 needs a strong second page to catch them, and it needs the comparison pages doing real work.

Third: it invites a comparison we lose. "Accountability plus goals plus reviews" puts us on the same shelf as Lattice, 15Five and Workday, none of which we beat on HRIS depth, and all of which have the logos and the compliance we do not. See [Lattice's "One Platform. No Trade-Offs." †](https://mobbin.com/sites/sections/03c23d5f-814f-4920-b230-77525af64176) for how completely that shelf is already occupied.

Fourth, and the one I would watch: the register can read as surveillance. "Every owner has a number" is what a COO wants to hear and what an employee dreads, and our champion has to sell this internally to the people who will use it daily. The copy has to stay on accountability and clarity and never drift into monitoring.

Fifth, practical: it needs a display typeface, a drawn figure and real photography. It is the most expensive of the three to make, and it fails ugly if the craft is not there. A restrained page executed at eighty percent looks unfinished, whereas a dense page executed at eighty percent just looks dense.

# Recommendation

**Build Direction 3, with Direction 1's proof ladder as its lower half.** The reason is a matter of what we actually have. Direction 1 leads with evidence we cannot yet produce: no Fortune 100 logos, no completed audit on the page, no analyst placement, no attributable customer number, and a half-filled proof ladder reads worse than none because every serious buyer knows the shape it should have. Direction 2 leads with screenshots of a product that is changing weekly across nine build phases, and even at its best it argues parity with ClickUp, which is the argument we lose by default because they got there first and are free. Direction 3 leads with the one asset that exists today and that no rival can copy without rebuilding their data model: work attached to the person accountable, the standard it must meet and the goal it moves. That is a real difference, it is the reason the product was built, and it is the only sentence in this document that Monday, ClickUp and Asana cannot say back. So: lead on the idea, prove it with one honest dense product frame instead of a tour, and then run Direction 1's ladder underneath it in full, filling the rungs as the company earns them, starting with three named customers and one completed audit. Keep Direction 2 alive as the Product page and as the live-workspace commitment, because the tab-switcher over real screens is the correct way to show seven modules and we will need it there regardless. And move the replacement argument off the homepage entirely and onto a real comparison surface, the way every rival in this research already does.

# DECIDED, 2026-09-22

The founder delegated the choice and set the register: "you decide, don't make it cluttered, make it iconic like Apple, and use power words."

**Direction 3, the Chain of Ownership, built with Apple's restraint.** Not Direction 3 as the research drew it. The research reached for a dense editorial page: technical-manual figures, monospace leader labels, graph-paper grids, a long proof ladder under the fold. That is Linear's register, not Apple's, and the founder has rejected clutter twice now. So the argument is Direction 3's and the execution is Apple's.

## What "iconic like Apple" means here, concretely

It is a discipline, not a mood. Every rule below is a constraint a reviewer can fail the page on.

1. **One idea per screen.** Each section owns the viewport and says exactly one thing. If a section needs two headlines it is two sections.
2. **Few words, enormous type.** Hero headline at or above 72px on desktop, six words or fewer. Subhead one sentence, never two. If a sentence can lose a word, it loses it.
3. **Whitespace is the design.** Generous margins above and below every element. Nothing crowds the headline. No section is dense enough to skim.
4. **One object per section, centred, large.** A product frame, a diagram, or a number. Never a grid of cards in the hero region. Never three things competing.
5. **No logo wall, no badge row, no feature grid above the fold.** The proof ladder still exists (Direction 1's lower half) but it lives far down and stays quiet.
6. **Power words, plain words.** Short, declarative, concrete. "Every task has an owner." Not "Comprehensive accountability infrastructure." No cleverness that only survives in a deck. Verbs over nouns.
7. **Motion is one move.** A section may do one thing on entry. Nothing loops, nothing parallaxes, nothing competes with reading.
8. **Colour is almost absent.** Near-black on white, one blue, used once per screen at most. All other colour lives inside the product frame where it already belongs.

## The spine

Ownership, stated, then shown, then proved:

1. Hero: the claim, in six words, over one product frame.
2. The problem, in one sentence, with one number.
3. The chain: task, owner, standard, goal. Four beats, one screen each, each with the real surface behind it.
4. The moment it pays off: the quarter ends and the record already exists.
5. The rest of the product, quietly: one line per module, no replaces lines, no grid of cards competing for attention.
6. The proof ladder, honest and small: what is true today, nothing more.
7. One closing line. One button.

## What does NOT change

The product's own chrome wherever the product appears: navy rail and top bar, white canvas, light-grey sidebar, Inter, one blue primary. The site may be as spare as it likes around the product; it may not restyle the product.

And the truth gates hold. The previous build shipped a fabricated compliance claim and six invented cookie rows, caught only by its own truth reviewer. Nothing on this site claims a customer, a certification, a number or a capability that does not exist.
